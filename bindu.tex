%\lettrine{D}{as} \textit{Tattvayogabindu} (``Die Essenz des Yoga und der Realität'') von Rāmacandra ist ein frühneuzeitlicher Sanskrit-Text zum Thema Rājayoga, der in der ersten Hälfte des siebzehnten Jahrhunderts verfasst wurde. Das vordergründig auffälligste Merkmal dieses Werkes ist seine hochdifferenzierte Taxonomie verschiedener Yogas. In der Einleitung des \textit{Tattvayogabindu} nennen die meisten Handschriften fünfzehn verschiedene Yogakategorien, die als Methoden des Rājayoga eingeführt werden. Diese lauten wie folgt: 1. Kriyāyoga, 2. Jñānayoga, 3. Caryāyoga, 4. Haṭhayoga, 5. Karmayoga, 6. Layayoga, 7. Dhyānayoga, 8. Mantrayoga, 9. Lakṣyayoga, 10. Vāsanāyoga, 11. Śivayoga, 12. Brahmayoga, 13. Advaitayoga, 14. Siddhayoga und 15. Rājayoga selbst.\footnote{Dies ist ein auffälliger Anstieg der Anzahl der deklarierten Yogas im Vergleich zu der mittelalterlichen Standard-Tetrade von Mantra-, Laya-, Haṭha- und Rājayoga.} Der Text ähnelt einem Kompendium, das in einer Mischung aus hauptsächlich Prosa und 47 Versen im Lehrbuchstil verfasst ist, wobei die 59 Themen des Textes in Abschnitte unterteilt sind, die zumeist durch erkennbare Phrasen eingeleitet werden. Die Abschnitte befassen sich mit den Methoden des Rājayoga und ihren Wirkungen, aber auch mit Themen wie der yogischen Physiologie, dem Avadhūta, der Bedeutung des Gurus, einer Kosmogonie und einem \textit{yogaśāstrarahasya}.
%
%Das \textit{Tattvayogabindu} wurde in der wissenschaftlichen Sekundärliteratur über Yoga bisher nicht ausführlich diskutiert. Die einzige Ausnahme bildet \citeauthor{birch2014} (2014: 415-416), der die Taxonomie der fünfzehn Yogas im Kontext der ``fünfzehn mittelalterlichen Yogas'' kurz beschreibt und feststellt, dass eine ähnliche Taxonomie in Nārāyaṇatīrthas \textit{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā} (17. Jh.) vorkommt, einem Kommentar zum \textit{Pātañjalayogaśāstra}, der fünfzehn Yogakategorien in das \textit{aṣṭāṅga}-Format integriert. Eine andere Darstellung von fünfzehn Yogas findet sich in einem weiteren Sanskrit-Yogatext namens \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, der nur durch Zitate in der \textit{Prāṇatoṣiṇī}, der \textit{Yogakarṇikā} und dem \emph{Śabdakalpadruma} überliefert ist. Das \textit{Yogasvarodaya} nennt zwar ebenfalls insgesamt fünfzehn Yogas, aber nur acht von ihnen in seinen einleitenden \textit{śloka}s. Das \textit{Yogasvarodaya} war der Hauptquelltext und die wichtigste Textvorlage für Rāmacandras Kompilation des \textit{Tattvayogabindu}. Abgesehen von einigen Passagen folgt Rāmacandra in vielen Fällen dessen Inhalt und Struktur, indem er die Verse des \textit{Yogasvarodaya} in Prosa umschreibt oder diese direkt ohne Zuschreibung zitiert. Aufgrund der unvollständigen Überlieferung des \textit{Yogasvarodaya} ist Rāmacandras \textit{Tattvayogabindu} ein wichtiger Ausgangspunkt für eine eingehende Untersuchung der komplexen frühneuzeitlichen Yogataxonomien, ein Phänomen, das sich zeitlich und, wie ich zeigen werde, auch räumlich sehr genau eingrenzen lässt. Der andere Quelltext, den Rāmacandra verwendete, ist die \textit{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati}, auf deren Inhalt er vor allem in der zweiten Hälfte seiner Komposition zurückgreift. Ein weiterer Text, der eine ähnliche Taxonomie von zwölf Yogas enthält, die in drei Tetraden unterteilt sind, ist Sundardās' in \textit{Brajbhāṣa} verfasster Yogatext mit dem Titel \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}. 
%
%Diese komplexen Yogataxonomien, die alle im 17. Jahrhundert entstanden, entsprangen sehr unterschiedlichen religiösen Strömungen und wurden von den Autoren der Texte an die spezifischen Bedürfnisse ihrer Traditionen angepasst. Das \textit{Tattvayogabindu} umfasst einen großen Teil der Vielfalt der Yogaformen, die im 17. Jh. von einem breiten Spektrum religiöser Traditionen und Schichten der indischen Gesellschaft praktiziert und diskutiert wurden. Im besonderen Fall des \emph{Tattvayogabindu} gibt es zahlreiche Aussagen im gesamten Text, die eine Strategie offenbaren, den Yoga von seinen typisch asketisch-weltentsagenden Konnotationen zu lösen und Rājayoga als eine Praxis zu stilisieren, die selbst für Praktizierende, die weltliche Vergnügungen und einen extravaganten Lebensstil genießen, den erstrebten soteriologischen Nutzen bringen kann. Textimmanente Belege deuten darauf hin, dass das \textit{Tattvayogabindu} ein wichtiges Beispiel für einen Text ist, der eine frühneuzeitliche Adaption des Rājayoga für ein \textit{kṣatriya}-Publikum in einem höfischen Umfeld darstellt.
%
%Eine Druckausgabe des \textit{Tattvayogabindu} wurde 1905 mitsamt einer Hindi-Übersetzung veröffentlicht und basiert auf einem unbekannten Manuskript.\footnote{\emph{Binduyoga}.\textit{Binduyogaḥ with Bhāṣaṭīkā}. Hrsg. von Jvālāprasāda Miśra. Mumbai, 1905.} Diese Veröffentlichung trägt den Titel ``\textit{Binduyoga}'', was durch das Kolophon des gedruckten Textes bestätigt wird. Wie im Verlauf der Einleitung erörtert wird, war der Text jedoch ursprünglich als \textit{Tattvayogabindu} bekannt. Die konsultierten Manuskripte weisen erhebliche Diskrepanzen, strukturelle Unterschiede und zahlreiche voneinander abweichende Lesarten zwischen ihnen und der gedruckten Ausgabe auf.\footnote{Beispielsweise enthält die Druckausgabe die in den Handschriften präsentierte komplexe Yogataxonomie nicht.} Diese Manuskripte sind vor allem über die nördliche Hälfte des indischen Subkontinents und Nepal verstreut, was darauf schließen lässt, dass der Text weit verbreitet war. Längere Passagen des \textit{Tattvayogabindu} werden ohne Quellenangabe in einem Text namens \textit{Yogasaṃgraha} und Sundaradevas \textit{Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā} zitiert. 
%
%Das erste Kapitel dieser Dissertation beinhaltet eine allgemeine Einführung in Rāmacandras \textit{Tattvayogabindu}. Das Kapitel gibt einen Kurzüberblick über den Inhalt des Textes, befasst sich mit dessen Herkunft, dem Autor und diskutiert das vom Autor intendierte Publikum seines Werkes. Weiterhin werden die Textzeugen, die Quelltexte und Testimonien des \textit{Tattvayogabindu} beschrieben. Im Anschluss wird eine stemmatische Analyse des Textes präsentiert, welche auf manueller philologischer Beobachtung und computergestützter Stemmatik basiert, um ein \textit{stemma codicum} zu präsentieren. Das Kapitel schließt mit einer Darlegung der Editionsrichtlinien ab, welche die Grundlage für das zweite Kapitel dieser Arbeit bildet.
%
%Das zweite Kapitel, das Herzstück dieser Arbeit, ist eine kritische Edition und annotierte Übersetzung des \textit{Tattvayogabindu}. Die kritische Edition dieser Dissertation verbessert den Text signifikant und wirft ein völlig neues Licht auf dessen historische Bedeutung.
%
%Das dritte Kapitel dieser Arbeit beinhaltet eine auf Differenzhermeneutik\footnote{Der Begriff Differenzhermeneutik wird auf S. \pageref{hermeneutics}, Fn. \ref{hemerneutics} näher erläutert.} basierende komparatistische Analyse der komplexen frühneuzeitlichen Yogataxonomien. Anhand der neuen kritischen Edition des \textit{Tattvayogabindu} und der weiter oben genannten Texte, \emph{Yogasvarodaya}, \emph{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā} und \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}, werden die vier komplexen Yogataxonomien detailliert miteinander verglichen. Anhand dieser Komparatistik wurde eine differenzierte Hypothese zur Entstehung der komplexen Yogataxonomien entwickelt und die komplexen Yogataxonomien im breiteren Kontext der historischen Entwicklung der Yogatraditionen verortet. Der Vergleich beinhaltet eine nuancierte Beschreibung der einzelnen von den Yogatraditionen verwendeten Yogakategorien. Zwar operieren die Autoren der vier Texte oftmals mit identischen Bezeichnungen für die einzelnen Yogakategorien, die sie in ihren Taxonomien auflisten, deuten diese Kategorien jedoch in vielen Fällen entsprechend ihres eigenen religiösen Hintergrundes und ihrer eigenen Agenda mit verblüffenden und interessanten Unterschieden aus. Die Kontrastierung der Komparanden, d.h. der Autoren, der Texte, der Yogataxonomien und der zur Verwendung kommenden Yogakategorien, eröffnet daher einen tiefen Einblick in die diskursiven Aushandlungsprozesse der indischen Yogatraditionen des 17. Jahrhunderts.

\section{Aṣṭāṅgayoga\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga}}
\phantomsection\label{ashtangayogacomplex}

\lettrine{A}{ṣṭāṅgayoga}\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga}, the ``eight-limbed yoga'' is invariably incorporated by all four authors who present complex yoga taxonomies in their texts, yet in markedly different ways. Rāmacandra\index{Ramacandra@Rāmacandra} does not mention Aṣṭāṅgayoga within his enumeration of the fifteen methods for Rājayoga\index{Rajayoga@Rājayoga} but introduces it in a separate section of his text.
e
The author of the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}\index{Yogasvarodaya@\textit{Yogasvarodaya}} presents an incomplete enumeration of the fifteen yogas, naming only eight of them and excluding Aṣṭāṅgayoga by name. Like Rāmacandra, however, he presents Aṣṭāṅgayoga elsewhere in the text. The introductory verse therefore leaves seven of the fifteen methods unaccounted for; although Aṣṭāṅgayoga is not named in the list, it is one of the most plausible candidates for inclusion among them. Its status as one of the fifteen methods of Rājayoga nevertheless remains uncertain. Rāmacandra, whose compilation relies heavily on the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, does not fully preserve this aspect of his source's taxonomy: he likewise treats Aṣṭāṅgayoga in a separate section, while the relationship between this section and his own fifteenfold scheme is not made explicit.

In contrast, Nārāyaṇatīrtha\index{Narayanatirtha@Nārāyaṇatīrtha} does not include Aṣṭāṅgayoga among his fifteen methods for achieving Rājayoga. Strictly speaking, Aṣṭāṅgayoga is not one of Nārāyaṇatīrtha's methods for Rājayoga. Nevertheless, he situates his fifteen yogas within the \textit{Pātañjalayogaśāstra}\index{Patanjalayogasastra@\textit{Pātañjalayogaśāstra}}, the \textit{locus classicus} of Aṣṭāṅgayoga; the eight-limbed framework thus provides the broader structure within which his fifteen yogas are situated in the \textit{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā}.\footnote{Due to its absence from Nārāyaṇatīrtha's fifteen yogas, and because the Aṣṭāṅgayoga of the \textit{Pātañjalayogaśāstra} has been extensively discussed in secondary literature, a renewed discussion is omitted here. Nārāyaṇatīrtha presents the eight limbs in his commentary on \textit{sūtra} 2.29 as follows: \textit{yamaniyamāsanaprāṇāyāmapratyāhāradhāraṇādhyānasamādhayo 'ṣṭāvaṅgāni} || 29 || \textit{yameti} | \textit{tatra yamāḥ svata eva saphalatvādatyāvaśyakāḥ} | \textit{sarvamumukṣujanasevyā ādau, paścāt tatsāpekṣā niyamāḥ} | \textit{etad ubhayādhīna cittasthairyasāpekṣāṇyāsanāni} | \textit{tatsāpekṣaḥ prāṇāyāmaḥ} | \textit{tatsāpekṣaḥ pratyāhāraḥ} | \textit{tatsāpekṣā dhāraṇā} | \textit{tatsāpekṣaṃ dhyānam} | \textit{tatsāpekṣaḥ samādhiḥ savikalpa ity aṣṭau sākṣāt paramparayā vā nirvikalpasya samādher aṅgānīty arthaḥ} | \textit{aṣṭāṅgamaithune kriyāniṣpatteraṅgino 'ṅgatvavat samādher aṅgino 'ṅgatvaṃ vā bodhyam} | \textit{tena na ko 'pi doṣaḥ} || 29 || In summary, Nārāyaṇatīrtha states that the limbs, beginning with the \textit{yama}s\index{yama@\textit{yama}}, build upon each other and culminate in \textit{samādhi}\index{samadhi@\textit{samādhi}}, which he also calls Rājayoga at the beginning of his commentary.}

Sundardās\index{Sundardas@Sundardās}'s \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}\index{Sarvangayogapradipika@\textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}} presents a starkly different scenario. Here, Aṣṭāṅgayoga is the last of the four methods of Haṭhayoga\index{Hathayoga@Haṭhayoga}.

\subsection{Aṣṭāṅgayoga\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga} in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu} and \textit{Yogasvarodaya}}
\phantomsection\label{ashtangayogax}
Since Rāmacandra's compilation of his Aṣṭāṅgayoga section largely follows the \textit{Yogasvarodaya},\footnote{Additionally, influences from the \emph{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati}\index{Siddhasiddhantapaddhati@\textit{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati}} 2.32–38 can be identified in this passage.} yet differs at several points, it is useful to compare the two texts.\footnote{See \emph{Tattvayogabindu} section \uproman{31} and \textit{Yogasvarodaya} (PT, p. 841).}
Both texts begin with an enumeration of the limbs of Aṣṭāṅgayoga. Notably, the \emph{Yogasvarodaya} lists only seven of the eight limbs, omitting \textit{dhyāna}\index{dhyana@\textit{dhyāna}}.\footnote{\textit{Yogasvarodaya} (PT, p. 841): \textit{idānīṃ yogamaṣṭāṅgaṃ śṛṇu lakṣaṇasaṃyutam} | \textit{yamaś ca niyamaś caiva cāsanaṃ prāṇasaṃyamaḥ} | \textit{pratyāhāro dhāraṇā ca samādhiś ca viśeṣataḥ} |} Intriguingly, subsequent verses describe \textit{dhyāna} but not \textit{dhāraṇā}\index{dharana@\textit{dhāraṇā}}. The \textit{Tattvayogabindu} lists all eight limbs, but Rāmacandra uses an order different from the Pātañjalayoga\index{Patanjalayoga@Pātañjalayoga} model, presenting \textit{dhyāna} before \textit{dhāraṇā}.\footnote{See n.~\ref{ashtangareversed} on p.~\pageref{ashtangareversed} for a discussion and further references on the reversed order of the limbs of Aṣṭāṅgayoga.} However, following his source, Rāmacandra omits subsequent explanations of \textit{dhāraṇā}. He also does not explain \emph{samādhi}.
Both texts provide detailed lists of six \textit{yama}s and \textit{niyama}s\index{niyama@\textit{niyama}}. Variations in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu} reflect the influence of the \emph{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati}, as well as Rāmacandra's own modifications, which appear to adapt the inherited material to his courtly audience and the broader aims of his compilation.\footnote{The \textit{yama}s of \textit{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati} 2.32 are: calmness (\textit{upaśamaḥ}\index{upasama@\textit{upaśama}}), conquest of all senses (\textit{sarvendriyajayaḥ}\index{sarvendriyajaya@\textit{sarvendriyajaya}}) and conquest of food, sleep, cold, wind, and heat (\textit{āhāranidrāśītavātātapajayaḥ}\index{aharanidrasitavatatapajaya@\textit{āhāranidrāśītavātātapajaya}}). The \textit{niyama}s of the \textit{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati} 2.33 are: living in solitude (\textit{ekāntavāsa}\index{ekantavasa@\textit{ekāntavāsa}}), detachment (\textit{niḥsaṅgatā}\index{nihsangata@\textit{niḥsaṅgatā}}), indifference (\textit{udāsīnyaṃ}\index{udasinya@\textit{udāsīnya}}), contentment with what is obtained (\textit{yathāprāptisaṃtuṣṭiḥ}\index{yathapraptisamtusti@\textit{yathāprāptisaṃtuṣṭi}}), aversion (\textit{vairasyaṃ}\index{vairasya@\textit{vairasya}}), and dedication to the feet of the guru (\textit{gurucaraṇāvarūḍhatvam}\index{gurucaranavarudhatva@\textit{gurucaraṇāvarūḍhatva}}).} For the similarities and differences between the \textit{yama}s, see table \ref{tab-yama}, and for the \textit{niyama}s, see table \ref{tab-niyama}. Notably, both texts omit non-violence (\emph{ahiṃsā}\index{ahimsa@\textit{ahiṃsā}}) and sexual abstinence (\textit{brahmacarya}\index{brahmacarya@\textit{brahmacarya}}). This omission is particularly noteworthy in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, whose intended audience appears to have included members of the royal court: the absence of these two restraints is consistent with a presentation of yoga that does not require the renunciant constraints of non-violence and celibacy.

The discussion of postures (\textit{āsana}s\index{asana@\textit{āsana}}) in both texts is brief. The \emph{Yogasvarodaya} addresses \textit{āsana} in half a verse, stating that there are as many postures as there are living beings.\footnote{\textit{Yogasvarodaya} (PT, p. 841): \textit{āsanāni ca tāvanti yāvanto jīvajantavaḥ} | The author of this verse alludes to the numerous animal names of many yoga positions. See e.g. \citetitle{encyclopediaasana} of \citeauthor{encyclopediaasana} (2006).} Rāmacandra explains that the characteristics of postures are discussed in many other treatises. He therefore does not cover this topic.\footnote{\textit{Tattvayogabindu} section \uproman{31}: \textit{āsanasya lakṣaṇaṃ bahūgrantheṣu nirūpitam asti} | \textit{tenātra na nirūpyate} |}

The \textit{Yogasvarodaya} states that breath control (\textit{prāṇāyāma}\index{pranayama@\textit{prāṇāyāma}}) is of three types and takes various forms. It asserts that young people are not capable of practising \textit{āsana} and \textit{prāṇāyāma}, but through the great power of spiritual merit (\textit{mahāpuṇyaprabhāva}\index{mahapunyaprabhava@\textit{mahāpuṇyaprabhāva}}), the great soul (\textit{mahātmā}\index{mahatma@\textit{mahātmā}}) can do so.\footnote{\textit{Yogasvarodaya} (PT, p. 841): \textit{prāṇāyāmas tridhā ceti bahudhā prathamaṃ śṛṇu} | \textit{āsane prāṇasaṃyāme na śaktāḥ sukumārakāḥ} | \textit{mahāpuṇyaprabhāveṇa śakyate tu mahātmanā} |} The author explains only the first type of \textit{prāṇāyāma}, which is a standard form of alternate nostril breathing accompanied by visualisation. The \textit{mahātmā} inhales through the left channel (\textit{iḍā}\index{Ida@\textit{Iḍā}}), i.e., the left nostril, meditating on its moonlike appearance. Then he holds his breath (\textit{kumbhaka}\index{kumbhaka@\textit{kumbhaka}}) for as long as possible. Once the mind is filled with great light and the body with air, he should exhale the breath, having overcome the fear caused by its force, while meditating on the sun-coloured right channel (\textit{piṅgalā}\index{Pingala@\textit{Piṅgalā}}) and releasing the breath slowly. The text compares this repeated process to a moth repeatedly approaching a wood fire and concludes by instructing the great-souled practitioner to purify the body through effort.\footnote{\textit{Yogasvarodaya} (PT, p. 841): \textit{iḍāṃ śaśiprabhāṃ dhyātvā mandendunā tu pūrayet} | \textit{pūrayitvā yathāśakti dhyānayogī tu kumbhayet} | \textit{mahājyotirmano bhūtvā vāyuḥpūrṇakalevaraḥ} | \textit{śaktitrāsan tu santrāsya recayed vāyum arhitaḥ} | \textit{piṅgalām arkavarṇān tu tyajed dhyātvā śanaiḥ śanaiḥ} | \textit{ayaṃ pataṅgaḥ kāṣṭhāgnipratyāsena punaḥ punaḥ} | \textit{kṛtvā kalevaraṃ śuddhaṃ kuryād yatnair mahātmanā} |}

Rāmacandra only mentions that \textit{prāṇāyāma} is unsuitable for young practitioners (\textit{sukūmāra}\index{sukumara@\textit{sukumāra}}), thus omitting any explanation of its practice.\footnote{\textit{Tattvayogabindu} section \uproman{31}: \textit{prāṇāyāmas tu sukumāreṇa sādhituṃ na śakyate} | \textit{atas tasya nāmamātraṃ kathyate} |} If Rāmacandra's statement is taken literally, it may indicate his intended audience. Why would he not describe a single breathing technique in his entire text?\footnote{For a discussion of the intended audience, see p.~\pageref{tyb-audience}.}
Both texts briefly explain the withdrawal of the senses (\textit{pratyāhāra}\index{pratyahara@\textit{pratyāhāra}}). In the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, \textit{pratyāhāra} entails withdrawing the mind from cyclical existence (\textit{saṃsāra}\index{samsara@\textit{saṃsāra}}) and from engagement with sensory objects. The resulting state is characterised by an empty inner organ. Similarly, in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, the yogin should turn his mind away from cyclical existence and dwell in the self, thereby restraining the emerging transformations of the mind.

The description of meditation (\textit{dhyāna}) is similarly concise in both texts. The \textit{Yogasvarodaya} mentions two types of meditation: a gross one consisting of \textit{mantra}\index{mantra@\textit{mantra}} (\textit{mantramaya}) and a subtle one without \textit{mantra}. Further details are not provided. The \textit{Tattvayogabindu} merely states that meditation has been taught many times before\footnote{Probably Rāmacandra refers to the descriptions of \emph{dhyāna} in his own text. See p. \pageref{ramacandradhyana} for the references.} and is therefore not discussed.\footnote{\textit{Tattvayogabindu} section \uproman{31}: \textit{dhyānaṃ ca bahutaraṃ prāg uktaṃ tenātra nocyate} |}

In the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, \textit{samādhi} is the state of the motionless intellect (\textit{buddhi}\index{buddhi@\textit{buddhi}}), free from inhalation and exhalation. Rāmacandra does not mention \textit{samādhi} at all in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}. At first glance, this seems peculiar, as the reader might expect a commentary on the supreme and final state of yoga. However, if Rāmacandra addresses young practitioners (\textit{sukūmāra}s), it is entirely understandable why he does not recommend that they cease breathing.\footnote{\emph{Prāṇatoṣiṇī}, quoted with reference to the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} (Ed. p. 841), defines \textit{samādhi} as follows: \textit{samādhir niścalā buddhiḥ śvāsocchvāsādivarjitaḥ} | ``\textit{Samādhi} is the immovable intellect devoid of inhalation, exhalation, etc.''}.

\subsection{Aṣṭāṅgayoga\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga} in the \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}}

For Sundardās, Aṣṭāṅgayoga (3.37–52) is the final method in his four-method system of Haṭhayoga.\footnote{Already in the \emph{Śārṅgadharapaddhati}\index{Sarngadharapaddhati@\textit{Śārṅgadharapaddhati}} 4372–4375, a six-limbed yoga system, a variant without \textit{yama} and \textit{niyama}, is subordinated to Haṭhayoga: \textit{atha haṭhayogaḥ dvidhā haṭhaḥ syād ekas tu gorakṣādisusādhitaḥ} | \textit{anyo mṛkaṇḍaputrādyaiḥ sādhito 'niśam udyataiḥ} ||1|| \textit{tatra gorakṣasadṛśaiḥ sādhito yaḥ sa kathyate} | \textit{dhīrair api hi duḥsādhyaḥ kiṃ punaḥ prākṛtair janaiḥ} ||2|| \textit{āsanaṃ prāṇasaṃrodhaḥ pratyāhāraś ca dhāraṇā} | \textit{dhyānaṃ samādhir etāni yogāṅgāni smṛtāni ṣaṭ} ||3||} It supplements the preceding methods: Haṭhayoga (which covers the practice environment, dietary rules, and \textit{ṣaṭkarma}s\index{satkarma@\textit{ṣaṭkarma}}), Rājayoga (\textit{vajrolīmudrā}\index{vajrolimudra@\textit{vajrolīmudrā}} as a means of preserving celibacy), and Lakṣayoga\index{Laksyayoga@Lakṣyayoga} (foci for meditation), thereby providing the entire \textit{haṭha} practice with a framework. It is precisely here, within the context of Aṣṭāṅgayoga that most of the characteristic haṭhayogic practices, namely \textit{āsana}s, \textit{kumbhaka}s, \textit{mudrā}s\index{mudra@\textit{mudrā}}, and \textit{bandha}s\index{bandha@\textit{bandha}}, are introduced. The order of the eight limbs follows the Pātañjalayoga model.

Sundardās first mentions the two initial limbs, the restraints \textit{yama} and observances \textit{niyama}, each with ten different aspects, which he does not list.\footnote{\textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} 3.37cd: \textit{prathamahiṃ yama aru niyama bicārai} | \textit{palari ṭeka daśa daśahiṃ prakārai} || 37 || This omission may be due to Sundardās's earlier treatment of many typical elements of \textit{yama} and \textit{niyama}; see p.~\pageref{sarvangabhakti}.}

The third limb, the practice of postures (\textit{āsana}), should be performed regularly to purify the body. For Sundardās, the two most important \textit{āsana}s are the accomplished posture (\textit{siddhāsana}\index{siddhasana@\textit{siddhāsana}}) and the lotus posture (\textit{padmāsana}\index{padmasana@\textit{padmāsana}}).\footnote{Ibid. 3.38: \textit{bahuryau karai su āsana sabahī} | \textit{nirma śarīra hoi puni tabahī} | \textit{tāmahiṃ sārabhūta dvai sādhai} | \textit{siddhāsana padmāsana baṃdhai} || 38 ||}
In the context of breath control (\textit{prāṇāyāma}), Sundardās initially emphasises that these exercises must be learned from a guru\index{guru@\textit{guru}}. He then describes the basic form of alternate nostril breathing. This involves inhaling through the left nostril (\textit{iḍā nāḍī}\index{nadi@\textit{nāḍī}!Iḍā}), followed by a retention phase, and then exhaling through the right nostril (\textit{piṅgalā nāḍī}\index{nadi@\textit{nāḍī}!Piṅgalā}). During the retention phases, one should mentally recite the mantra twelve times. At this point, no further details about the mantra are provided. The unspecified mantra may be the \textit{Rāma mantra}\index{Rama mantra@\textit{Rāma mantra}} mentioned in Sundardās's Mantrayoga (2.16–27). The verse then refers to doubled and tripled forms of \textit{prāṇāyāma}, and names three grades: upper (\textit{uttama}\index{uttama@\textit{uttama}}), middle (\textit{madhyama}\index{madhyama@\textit{madhyama}}), and lower (\textit{kaniṣṭa}\index{kanistha@\textit{kaniṣṭa}}). The precise correspondence between the two numerical multipliers and the three grades, however, is not made explicit.\footnote{Ibid. 3.39–40: \textit{prāṇāyāma karai bibhi aisī} | \textit{sataguru saṃdhi batāvai jaisī} | \textit{iḍā nāḍi kati pūrai bāī} | \textit{recaka karai piṃgalā jāī} || 39 || \textit{pūri piṃgalā iḍā nikārai} | \textit{dvādaśa vāra mantra bidhi dhārai} | \textit{dviguṇa triguṇa kari prāṇāyāmaṃ} | \textit{uttama madhyama kaniṣṭa nāmaṃ} || 40 ||} Additionally, Sundardās mentions the eight breath retentions (\textit{kumbhaka}s) and five types of seals (\textit{mudrā}s), as well as the three locks (\textit{bandha}s). Further distinctions are to be learned from the guru.\footnote{Ibid. 3.41: \textit{kuṃbhaka aṣṭa bhāṃti ke jānaiṃ} | \textit{mudrā paṃca prakāra su ṭhānaiṃ} | \textit{baṃdha tīni nīkī bidhi lāvai} | \textit{aura bheda sadaguru taiṃ pāvai} || 41 ||} Sundardās does not offer detailed descriptions of these practices.
Regarding the fifth limb, the withdrawal of the senses (\textit{pratyāhāra}), Sundardās is very brief. He defines \textit{pratyāhāra} as controlling the mind so that one never craves the ``taste of sensory objects'' (\textit{biṣai svāda}). He uses the metaphor of a turtle retracting its limbs into its shell to illustrate \textit{pratyāhāra}.\footnote{Ibid. 3.42: \textit{pratyāhāra pakari mana rāṣai} | \textit{biṣai svāda kabahūṃ nahiṃ cāṣai} | \textit{jaisaiṃ kurama sakucai aṃgā} | \textit{esaiṃ indrī rāṣai saṃgā} || 42 ||}

Sundardās's sixth limb, concentration (\textit{dhāraṇā}), involves focusing on each of the five elements---earth (\textit{pṛthvī}\index{prthvi@\textit{pṛthvī}}), water (\textit{apa}\index{apas@\textit{apas}}), fire (\textit{teja}\index{tejas@\textit{tejas}}), air (\textit{vāyu}\index{vayu@\textit{vāyu}}), and ether (\textit{ākāśa}\index{akasa@\textit{ākāśa}})---and meditating on the deity associated with each. The verse appears to prescribe five \textit{ghaṭikā}\index{ghatika@\textit{ghaṭikā}}s for each elemental concentration.\footnote{One \textit{ghaṭikā} equals 1/60 of a day, see \citeauthor{sircar1966} (1966: 114). 1/60 of a day corresponds to 24 minutes. Five \textit{ghaṭikā}s equal exactly two hours.}\footnote{Ibid. 3.43: \textit{paṃca dhāraṇā tatva prakāśā} | \textit{pṛthi apa teja vāyu ākāśā} | \textit{akṣara sahita devatani dhyāvai} | \textit{paṃca paṃca ghaṭikā laya lāvai} || 43 ||}

Sundardās divides meditation (\textit{dhyāna}), the seventh limb of his Aṣṭāṅgayoga, into two categories: meditation with qualities (\textit{saguṇa}\index{saguna@\textit{saguṇa}}) and meditation without qualities (\textit{nirguṇa}\index{nirguna@\textit{nirguṇa}}). The former is directed towards the locations of the six \textit{cakra}s\index{cakra@\textit{cakra}}, while the latter is meditation on the formless self.\footnote{Ibid. 3.44: \textit{dhyāna su āhi ubhai ja prakāra} | \textit{eka saguṇa ika nirguna sārā} | \textit{saguna su kahiye cakra sthānaṃ} | \textit{nirguṇa rūpa ātamā dhyānaṃ} || 44 ||}

Verses 3.45–48 describe the system of six \textit{cakra}s, which is widely attested in medieval Haṭhayoga texts: \textit{ādhāra}\index{adhara@\textit{ādhāra}}, \textit{svādhiṣṭhāna}\index{svadhisthana@\textit{svādhiṣṭhāna}}, \textit{maṇipūra}\index{manipura@\textit{maṇipūra}}, \textit{anāhata}\index{anahata@\textit{anāhata}}, \textit{viśuddha}\index{visuddha@\textit{viśuddha}}, and \textit{ājñā}\index{ajna@\textit{ājñā}}. Sundardās concludes that meditation on the six \textit{cakra} locations leads to the recognition of the formless (\textit{nirguṇa}). The passage thus presents this recognition as a subsequent stage in the progression of meditation.\footnote{Ibid. 3.48: \textit{iti ṣaṭa cakra dhyāna jau tānai} | \textit{tabahiṃ jāī nirguṇa pahacānai} | \textit{gaganākāra dhyāya saba ṭhairā} | \textit{prabhā marīcī jala nahiṃ aurā} || 48 ||}

From the \textit{nirguṇa} stage of meditation arises the eighth limb, meditative absorption (\textit{samādhi}). Verse 3.49 compares this absorption to salt dissolving in water and describes the subsiding of the functions of mind and senses. Verse 3.50 then states that the individual self (\textit{jīvātman}\index{jivatman@\textit{jīvātman}}) and the supreme self (\textit{paramātmā}\index{paramatman@\textit{paramātman}}) become one, sharing the same essence (\textit{sama rasa}).\footnote{Ibid. 3.49–50: \textit{aba samādhi aisī bidhī karaī} | \textit{jaisaiṃ laiṃna nīra mahiṃ garaī} | \textit{mana indrī kī vṛtya samāvai} | \textit{tākau nāma samādhi kahāvai} || 49 || \textit{jīvātma paramātma doī} | \textit{sama rasa kari jaba ekai hoī} | \textit{bisarai āpa kachu nahiṃ jānai} | \textit{tākau nāma samadhi vaṣānai} || 50 ||} Sundardās characterises this state as follows:

\begin{quote}
\textit{kāla na ṣāi śastra nahiṃ lāgai} | \textit{yaṃtra maṃtra tā deṣata bhāgai} | \\
\textit{śīta uṣna kabahūṃ nahiṃ hoī} | \textit{parama sāmādhi kahāvai soī }|| 51 || 
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
Time cannot affect it and no weapon can violate it; \textit{yantra} and \textit{mantra} flee at the sight of it. It is not affected by cold or heat; this is called the supreme \textit{samādhi}.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Aṣṭāṅgayoga\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga} in the complex yoga taxonomies}

The comparative analysis of Aṣṭāṅgayoga across the four early modern yoga taxonomies reveals the diverse and nuanced uses of the term in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Despite shared terminology, three distinct models emerge.

Nārāyaṇatīrtha adopts the Pātañjala model as the basic framework for his presentation, while interpreting the limbs as successive and mutually dependent means to \textit{nirvikalpasamādhi}. Within this framework, he incorporates the popular yogas of his milieu.

By contrast, the Aṣṭāṅgayoga of the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} and \textit{Tattvayogabindu} reflects Śaiva\index{Saiva@Śaiva} milieus, indicated by the reversed order of \textit{dhyāna} and \textit{dhāraṇā} and by distinct readings of \textit{samādhi}. Structurally, these texts display the opposite configuration: rather than integrating other yogas into the Pātañjala model, they subsume Aṣṭāṅgayoga within a Śaiva hierarchy headed by a form of Rājayoga.

Sundardās’s \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} likewise embeds Aṣṭāṅgayoga within a progressive sequence of yogas: it is a subcategory of Haṭhayoga and occupies a preparatory position in relation to Advaitayoga\index{Advaitayoga}, the culminating goal of the system. This does not render the preceding yogas dispensable or inferior in themselves; rather, Sundardās integrates them into an interlocking progression in which each contributes to the yogic path. His limbs of Aṣṭāṅgayoga incorporate characteristic Haṭhayoga practices (\textit{mudrā}, \textit{bandha}) and structure a \textit{cakra} system, reinforcing the impression of a cohesive twelvefold whole.

Taken together, these models reveal how the negotiation between innovation and tradition could take different forms, from the incorporation of regional and popular practices into established frameworks to competition over taxonomic and interpretive authority. They thus illustrate the dynamic reshaping of yogic systems across traditions in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

\section{Sāṃkhyayoga\index{Samkhyayoga@Sāṃkhyayoga}}
\phantomsection\label{samkhyayoga}

\lettrine{S}{āṃkhyayoga}\index{Samkhyayoga@Sāṃkhyayoga}, the ``Yoga of the enumerative philosophical method'' appears only in Sundardās's \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} (4.1–12). None of the other texts containing complex early modern yoga taxonomies discusses Sāṃkhyayoga.\footnote{However, Sāṃkhyayoga also appears in \emph{Śivayogapradīpikā}\index{Sivayogapradipika@\textit{Śivayogapradīpikā}} 4.19–31. Here, it belongs to Rājayoga within the description of \textit{samādhi} and is thus embedded in the basic structure of an Aṣṭāṅgayoga.} Sundardās specifies Ṛṣabhadeva\index{Rsabhadeva@Ṛṣabhadeva}, Kapilamūni\index{Kapila}, Dattātreya\index{Dattatreya@Dattātreya}, Vaśiṣṭha\index{Vasistha@Vasiṣṭha}, Aṣṭāvakra\index{Astvakra@Aṣṭāvakra}, and Jaḍabharata\index{Jadabharata@Jaḍabharata} as important representatives of Sāṃkhyayoga.\footnote{\emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} 1.5: \textit{rṣabhadeva aru kapila mūni, dattātreya vaśiṣṭha} | \textit{aṣṭāvakra ru jaḍabharata, ina kai sāṃkhya sudṛṣṭa} || 5 ||} After Bhaktiyoga and Haṭhayoga, Sāṃkhyayoga is the third and final main category in Sundardās's yoga system. Sāṃkhyayoga forms the starting point for the three subsequent yogas associated with this category, namely Jñānayoga\index{Jnanayoga@Jñānayoga},\footnote{For the discussion of Jñānayoga in the \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}, see p.~\pageref{jnanayogaintrocandrika}.} Brahmayoga\index{Brahmayoga},\footnote{For the discussion of Brahmayoga in the \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}, see p.~\pageref{sundarbrahma}.} and Advaitayoga.\footnote{For the discussion of Advaitayoga in the \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā}, see p.~\pageref{sundaradvaita}.} Before the Yogi recognises unity with the world through Jñānayoga, experiences unity with the universe through Brahmayoga, and ultimately overcomes duality in Advaitayoga, Sāṃkhyayoga functions as an initial differentiation of the self from the non-self, a distinction that is progressively reworked and ultimately superseded in the subsequent stages.\footnote{\emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} 4.1: \textit{aba sāṃkhya su yoga hi suni lehū} | \textit{pīchai hamako doṣa na dehū} | \textit{ātama ana ātamā bicārā} | \textit{yāhī teṃ saṃkhya su nirddhārā} || 1 ||} Compared to classical Sāṃkhya\index{Samkhya@Sāṃkhya}, consciousness (\textit{puruṣa}\index{purusa@\textit{puruṣa}}) here (4.2–3) is referred to as the self (\textit{ātama}\index{atman@\textit{ātman}}) and primordial nature (\textit{prakṛti}\index{prakrti@\textit{prakṛti}}) as the non-self (\textit{anātama}\index{anatman@\textit{anātman}}):

\begin{quote}
\textit{ātama śuddha su nitya prakāśā} | \textit{ana ātamā deha kā nāśā} |\\
\textit{ātama sukṣma vyāpaka mūlā} | \textit{ana ātamā so paṃca sthūlā} || 2 || 
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
The self is pure, eternal and ever-luminous. The non-self is the perishable body. The self is subtle, omnipresent and the fundamental ground; the non-self is the five-fold gross [reality].
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
\textit{pṛthi apu teja vāyu aru gaganā} | \textit{ye paṃcauṃ ātama saṃlagnā} |\\
\textit{paṃcani maiṃ mila aura bikārā} | \textit{tini yaha kiyā prapaṃca pasārā} || 3 ||
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
Earth, water, fire, air and ether---these five are attached to the self. In these five elements, other transformations occur, and these three [self, non-self and transformations] have created the proliferation of the universe.
\end{quote}

The non-self consists of the five gross elements: earth (\textit{pṛthi}), water (\textit{apu}), fire (\textit{teja}), air (\textit{vāyu}), and ether (\textit{gaganā}); the five subtle elements: sound (\textit{śabda}\index{sabda@\textit{śabda}}), touch (\textit{sparśa}\index{sparsa@\textit{sparśa}}), form (\textit{rūpa}\index{rupa@\textit{rūpa}}), taste (\textit{rasa}\index{rasa@\textit{rasa}}), and smell (\textit{gandhā}\index{gandha@\textit{gandha}}); the five senses of perception (\textit{jñānendriya}s\index{jnanendriya@\textit{jñānendriya}}): ear (\textit{śrotra}), skin (\textit{tvak}), eye (\textit{cakṣu}), tongue (\textit{jihvā}), and nose (\textit{ghrāṇa}); the five organs of action: speech (\textit{vākya}), grasping (\textit{pāṇi}), locomotion (\textit{pāda}), excretion (\textit{pāyu}), and reproduction (\textit{upastha}); and the inner organ (\textit{antaḥkaraṇa}\index{antahkarana@\textit{antaḥkaraṇa}}) consisting of mind (\textit{mana}), intellect (\textit{buddhi}), consciousness (\textit{citta}\index{citta@\textit{citta}}), and ego (\textit{ahaṃkāra}\index{ahamkara@\textit{ahaṃkāra}}).\footnote{Ibid. 4.4–6: \textit{śabda saparśa rūpa rasa gaṃdhā} | \textit{tanmātṛkā paṃca tana baṃdhā} | \textit{śrotra tvak cakṣu jihvā ghrāṇaṃ} | \textit{jñāna su indriya kiyau baṣāṇaṃ} || 4 || \textit{vākya hi pāṇi pāda aru pāyuḥ} | \textit{upastha sahita paṃca samajñāyuḥ} | \textit{karma su indriya ina kau nāmā} | \textit{tatpara apanai apanai kāmā} || 5 || \textit{mana uru buddhi citta ahaṃkārā} | \textit{catuṣṭa antahakaraṇa vicārā} | \textit{tina kai lakṣaṇa bhinnai bhinnā} | \textit{mahāpuruṣa samujñāye cinhā} || 6 ||} These comprise twenty-four \textit{tattva}s\index{tattva@\textit{tattva}} in total.

Sundardās then elaborates on the inner organ. The thinking mind (\textit{manas}\index{manas@\textit{manas}}) generates thoughts and doubts. The intellect (\textit{buddhi}) discerns and recognises what is good and bad. Consciousness (\textit{citta}) generates awareness. The ego (\textit{ahaṃkāra}) produces self-awareness and pride.\footnote{Ibid. 4.7–8ab: \textit{saṃkalpai aru bikalapa karai} | \textit{mana so lakṣaṇa esau dharai} | \textit{buddhi su lakṣaṇa bodhahiṃ jāṃnī} | \textit{kīkai burau leī pahicānī} || 7 || \textit{caitana lakṣaṇa citta anūpā} | \textit{ahaṃkāra abhimāna svarūpā}~|}

Finally, Sundardās distinguishes the subtle transmigratory body (\textit{liṅga śarīra}\index{linga sarira@\textit{liṅga śarīra}}), composed of the five subtle elements and the inner organ, totalling nine \textit{tattva}s, from the gross body, which is subject to decay and comprises the gross elements, the senses of perception, and the organs of action.\footnote{Ibid. 4.8cd: \textit{nau tatvani kau liṃga śarīrā} | \textit{paṃdraha tatva sthūla gaṃbhīrā} || 8 ||}

Sundardās concludes by explaining that these twenty-four elements work together, with the soul (\textit{jīva}\index{jiva@\textit{jīva}}) as the impeller of these twenty-four elements. He also refers to the soul as the knower of the field (\textit{kṣetrajña}\index{ksetrajna@\textit{kṣetrajña}}) or eternally auspicious (\textit{nirantara śīvā}). The soul permeates everything and is omnipresent. It appears to be involved in everything but is ultimately unbound. As the witness, it is distinct from all else, the twenty-four \textit{tattva}s of the non-self. The self and the non-self are eternal and not subject to ageing and death. However, the gross body (\textit{deha}) is transient.\footnote{Ibid. 4.9–12: \textit{ye caubīsa tatva baṃdhānaṃ} | \textit{bhinna-bhinna karikiyau vaṣānaṃ} | \textit{saba kau preraka kahiye jīvā} | \textit{so kṣetajña nirantara śīvā} || 9 || \textit{sakala viyāpaka aru sarvagā} | \textit{dīsai saṃgī āhi asaṃgā} | \textit{sākṣī rūpa sabani teṃ nyārā} | \textit{tāhi kachū nahiṃ lipai bikārā} || 10 || \textit{yaha ātama ana ātama niranā} | \textit{sagajñai takauṃ jarā na maraṇā} | \textit{sāṃkhya su mata yāhī sauṃ kahiye} | \textit{sataguru binā kahauṃ kyauṃ lahiye} || 11 || \textit{sāṃkhya yoga so yaha kahyau, bhinna hi bhinna prakāra} | \textit{ātama nitya svarūpa hai, deha anitya vicāra} || 12 ||}

\section{Satyayoga\index{Satyayoga}}
\phantomsection\label{satyayoga}

\lettrine{S}{atyayoga}\index{Satyayoga}, the ``true yoga'' is a term that appears in the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} in \uproman{44}.7 in the section on \textit{avadhūtapuruṣasya lakṣaṇam}\index{Avadhuta@Avadhūta}:

\begin{quote}
  \textit{prasaraṃ bhāsate śaktiḥ saṃkocaṃ bhāsate 'pi ca} |\\
  \textit{tayoḥ saṃyogakartā yaḥ sa bhavet satyayogabhāk} ||
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
   Śakti\index{Sakti@Śakti} shines forth as expansion and as contraction. He who unites those two experiences true yoga.
  \end{quote}

  The term \textit{satyayoga}\index{Satyayoga} is not included in the initial taxonomy of the fifteen methods for Rājayoga. Rāmacandra adopted the verse from the \emph{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati} 6.64, redacted it for his own purposes, and combined it with material from \emph{Siddhasiddhāntapaddhati} 4.19.\footnote{See p.~\pageref{satyaavadhuta}.} All manuscripts of the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} read \textit{satyayogabhāk}\index{Satyayoga} in the fourth \textit{pāda} of the verse. Here, the source text reads \textit{siddhayogirāṭ}\index{Siddhayoga}. Rāmacandra may have deliberately used the term as a synonym for Siddhayoga\index{Siddhayoga}\footnote{For the discussion of Siddhayoga in the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, see p.~\pageref{siddhayogaintro}.}; alternatively, \textit{satyayogabhāk} may be a textual corruption of the source reading. According to the definition given in this verse, Satyayoga would be a practice in which the practitioner dedicates himself to the union of two aspects of Śakti, here defined as expansion (\textit{prasāra}\index{prasara@\textit{prasāra}}) and contraction (\textit{saṃkoca}\index{samkoca@\textit{saṃkoca}}). In the light of the source text, it could be a corruption of the term \textit{siddhayoga°}, possibly arising from the homoioteleutic ending of the preceding verse. Since all extant manuscripts of the \textit{Tattvayogabindu} share the reading \textit{satyayogabhāk}, any such corruption would have to predate their common ancestor. Within the medieval and premodern yoga texts surveyed here, I have not identified another text that uses Satyayoga to designate a distinct method of yoga. For this reason, it seems unlikely that the term represents a stable, independent category or method of yoga. 
  
\section{Sahajayoga\index{Sahajayoga}}
\phantomsection\label{sahajayoga}

\lettrine{S}{ahajayoga}\index{Sahajayoga} is mentioned only in the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} among the texts containing complex yoga taxonomies. Generally, in Sanskrit literature, the term is often linked to the ``classical'' Avadhūta\index{Avadhuta@Avadhūta}. The archetypal Avadhūta has renounced \textit{saṃsāra} and rejects both worldly and religious activities. He is typically associated with \textit{nirguṇa bhakti}\index{nirguna bhakti@\textit{nirguṇa bhakti}}, devotion to the formless Divine. In Mallinson's account, rather than pursuing complex spiritual disciplines, this Avadhūta follows the sole true and natural practice: the repetition of the Divine name.\footnote{\citeauthor[2011: 15]{mallinsonnath}.}

The model of the Avadhūta that emerges in Rāmacandra's \emph{Tattvayogabindu} can be read as an instance of the trend that \citeauthor{pudi2023} (2023: 18) calls the ``sanitisation'' of the Avadhūta. In her study, she describes the term's history and identifies a clear development. Over the centuries, the Avadhūta, the original antinomian ascetic, was gradually integrated into the Brahmanical \emph{āśrama}\index{asrama@\textit{āśrama}} system. His antinomian behaviour, unconventional traits, and unorthodox practice were tamed, and according to Kanamarlapudi, the Avadhūta became a legitimate and eventually even the highest class of the \textit{saṃnyāsa āśrama}\index{sannyasa asrama@\textit{saṃnyāsa āśrama}}.

An aspect of this development can be observed, for example, in the early history of Haṭhayoga. \citeauthor{mallinsonnath} (2011: 17) notes that in the early history of Haṭhayoga attributed to Gorakṣa\index{Goraksa@Gorakṣa} (eleventh–thirteenth centuries), ``the antinomian external practices of Tantra were internalised and, together with the visualisation techniques of \textit{kuṇḍalinīyoga}\index{Kundaliniyoga@Kuṇḍalinīyoga}, were melted with the physical practices of early Haṭha Yoga, whose original aim was the sublimation of sexual continence into liberation.''

Rāmacandra's presentation can be read as continuing this process of sanitisation, reshaping the Avadhūta for royal or courtly adoption. In the \emph{Tattvayogabindu}, the Avadhūta becomes the ideal type of the Rājayoga\index{Rajayoga@Rājayoga}-practising \emph{kṣatriya}\index{ksatriya@\textit{kṣatriya}} or king, as the final section's explicit address to a king confirms. Rāmacandra cuts off the last threads of the image of Avadhūta that is still connected to the antinomian ascetic.\footnote{\emph{Tattvayogabindu} \uproman{44} p. \pageref{avadhuta}.} By contrast, Rāmacandra's Avadhūta does not scorn worldly activity; rather, he is indifferent to it yet fully involved in it, and permits continued participation in worldly pleasures. The natural consequence is that Rāmacandra elevates Sahajayoga to the practice \textit{par excellence} for a king.  

Rāmacandra defines Sahajayoga, the ``natural yoga,'' at the very end of his work, in section \uproman{59}. This passage is presented as the ``esoteric yoga doctrine in all of the scriptures,'' forming the grand climax of the text. This secret is explicitly directed at a king and, as argued in the introduction, may be understood more broadly as addressing a courtly audience.\footnote{\emph{Tattvayogabindu} \uproman{59}: \textit{yasya rājño manomadhye kapaṭaṃ nāsti} | \textit{yasmin dṛṣṭe deśakasya trāso na bhavati} | \textit{yasya manaḥ śuddhaṃ bhavati} | \textit{yasya pṛthivyāṃ kīrtir bhavati} | \textit{yasya manomadhye satpuruṣavacanaviśvāso bhavati} | \textit{yo rājā sadānandapūrṇo bhavati} | \textit{yasya pārśve pratyakṣam anekaṃ manohārivastūni bhavanti} | \textit{etādṛśasya rājño 'gre yogarahasyaṃ kathanīyam} |} More specifically, the secret is addressed to a good king characterised by being honest, fearless, reputable, trusting of good people, always blissful, and surrounded by enchanting objects.

Then Rāmacandra explains to whom this secret should explicitly not be revealed: not to those who blame others, criticise, misbehave, do not speak the truth, lack compassion, and enjoy conflict.\footnote{Ibid. \uproman{59}: \textit{yaḥ paranindā rato bhavati} | \textit{dūrācāro bhavati} | \textit{bhrātumitrasya ca yogyaṃ vastu na dadāti} | \textit{yo 'satyaṃ vadati} | \textit{yo yogināṃ manomadhye nindāṃ karoti} | \textit{yasya manomadhye dayā na bhavati} | \textit{yaḥ kalahapriyo bhavati} | \textit{svakāryakaraṇe sāvadhāno bhavati} | \textit{guroḥ kāryakaraṇe 'nādito bhavati} | \textit{etādṛśasyāgre na yogaḥ kriyate na paṭhyate} |}

Finally, Rāmacandra reveals the secret. The esoteric yoga teaching describes a person who embodies the highest reality, free from existence and non-existence (\textit{bhāvābhāvavinirmuktaḥ}\index{bhavabhavavinirmukta@\textit{bhāvābhāvavinirmukta}}).\footnote{This statement implies a characterisation of the highest reality (\textit{tattva}), which in \emph{Amanaska}\index{Amanaska@\textit{Amanaska}} 2.62 is described as free from the duality of existence and non-existence: \textit{bhāvābhāvadvayātītaṃ svapnajāgaraṇātigam} | \textit{mṛtyujīvananirmuktaṃ tattvaṃ tattvavido viduḥ} || 62 || ``The knowers of the highest reality know that the highest reality is beyond the duality of existence and non-existence, passes beyond [both] sleep and waking and is free from dying and living.'' (Translated by \citeauthor[2013: 318]{birch2013}).} And although this person, viz. the king or a \textit{kṣatriya}, as the enjoyer of the earth, is constantly exposed to worldly pleasures, he is free from all attachments. This person is a yogin made of perpetual bliss (\textit{sadānandamayo yogī}\index{sadanandamaya@\textit{sadānandamaya}}) and dwells in constant equanimity towards happiness and sorrow. The person has realised the indivisible highest self and performs actions without personal desires or attachments.\footnote{\emph{Tattvayogabindu} \uproman{59}. 1–8.}

While an ordinary person must constantly stabilise the gaze (\textit{dṛṣṭiḥ sthirā kartavyā}), stabilise the sitting posture (\textit{āsanaṃ dṛḍhaṃ kartavyaṃ}), and stabilise the breath (\textit{pavanaḥ sthiraḥ kartavyaḥ}) to achieve this state, a perfected person no longer needs to adhere to this discipline (\textit{etādṛśaḥ kaścin niyamaḥ siddhasya noktaḥ} |).
\begin{quote}
  \textit{manaḥpavanābhyāṃ yadā sahajānandaḥ svasvarūpeṇa prakāśyate} | \textit{sa sahajayogaḥ kathyate rājayogamadhye} | \textit{iti cakravartināmakathanaṃ} |
\end{quote}\begin{quote}
 When by means of mind and breath, the natural bliss appears through one's own true nature, it is called Sahajayoga (``natural yoga'') [in this system] of Rājayoga. This is the explanation for those named \textit{cakravartin}\index{cakravartin@\textit{cakravartin}} (``Universal Ruler'').
\end{quote}

This is the core message of Rāmacandra's secret teaching on yoga in all scriptures. A comparable idea is found in the \textit{rājaguhyam}\index{rajaguhya@\textit{rājaguhya}} of the \emph{Mokṣopāya}, a teaching through which rulers could attain a state free from suffering.\footnote{\emph{Mokṣopāya}\index{Moksopaya@\textit{Mokṣopāya}} 2.11.10–17.\nocite{moskopaya12}} These words conclude Rāmacandra's \emph{Tattvayogabindu}. Thus, Sahajayoga is the ideal yoga for a king. For a king who rules and enjoys the amenities of this position, this is the ultimate state to which he aspires. As a \textit{kṣatriya}, he can maintain the soteriological state through Sahajayoga and continue fulfilling his caste duties without continuous practice. The duties of a ruler sometimes involve ``cruel'' actions, such as enforcing laws, possibly through war. Additionally, Sahajayoga enables the king, despite being an ``enjoyer of the earth,'' to achieve soteriological completion without the deprivations typical for ascetics.\footnote{See also \citeauthor[2006: 121]{hanneder2006}.} The term \textit{sahaja°}\index{sahaja@\textit{sahaja}} is here understood primarily as ``natural'' in the specific sense of ``preserving its original nature'' and ``not undergoing further change.''\footnote{\citetitle[1858: 99]{petersburger7}.} 
  
\section{Conclusion}
\phantomsection\label{comparisonconclusion}

\lettrine{T}{he} comparison of the medieval complex yoga taxonomies in the four texts \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, \textit{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā}, and \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} reveals a remarkable diversity of twenty-three yoga categories. These categories are contextualised and structured differently depending on the text and, in most cases, show significant differences even where they overlap.

\item Jñānayoga\index{Jnanayoga@Jñānayoga}
\item Caryāyoga\index{Caryayoga@Caryāyoga}
\item Carcāyoga\index{Carcayoga@Carcāyoga}
\item Haṭhayoga\index{Hathayoga@Haṭhayoga}
\item Karmayoga\index{Karmayoga}

\item Lakṣyayoga\index{Laksyayoga@Lakṣyayoga}
\item Vāsanāyoga\index{Vasanayoga@Vāsanāyoga}
\item Śivayoga\index{Sivayoga@Śivayoga}
\item Brahmayoga\index{Brahmayoga}
\item Advaitayoga\index{Advaitayoga}
\item Siddhayoga\index{Siddhayoga}
\item Siddhakuṇḍalinīyoga\index{Siddhakundaliniyoga@Siddhakuṇḍalinīyoga} 
\item Siddhiyoga\index{Siddhiyoga}
\item Aṣṭāṅgayoga\index{Astangayoga@Aṣṭāṅgayoga}

\end{enumerate}

Satyayoga is excluded from this count because, as argued above, the passage in which it occurs is more plausibly explained as an authorial substitution or a textual corruption than as evidence for a stable, independent yoga category.

The degree of difference in the taxonomies and the significantly divergent interpretations of the yoga categories among the texts indicate that transmission was not a purely textual phenomenon with a linear reception history. Instead, the complex yoga taxonomies may also have circulated through cross-traditional and overlapping oral discourses. This possibility is consistent with the fact that the authors of the texts containing these complex early modern yoga taxonomies come from different religious traditions. Nevertheless, it must be stated clearly that the \emph{Yogasvarodaya} (A) is, at present, the oldest known text in the corpus examined here to articulate a complex taxonomy of fifteen yogic methods. It constitutes the source text of the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} (B), which likewise presents a fifteenfold taxonomy, and must therefore predate both Nārāyaṇatīrtha’s \emph{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā} (C) and Sundardās’s \emph{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} (D). Given that the present investigation establishes only the relative chronology of texts exhibiting complex taxonomies, any reception-historical influence at the purely textual level---if such influence occurred at all---can be assumed to have followed the following simplified pattern: A → B → C ↔ D.

Rāmacandra, the author of the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, promotes a form of religious universalism oriented towards Advaita Vedānta\index{Advaita Vedanta@Advaita Vedānta}. Despite the many Śaiva features inherited from his source texts, his own presentation is deliberately non-sectarian, to the point of appearing almost profane: explicit religious affiliations are largely bracketed, perhaps to appeal to a kṣatriya audience. By contrast, the author of the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} likely emerged from a South Indian Śaiva\index{Saiva@Śaiva} milieu. Nārāyaṇatīrtha was a renowned \textit{saṃnyāsa} intellectual and prolific author, born a Brahmin\index{Brahmin} and a follower of Kṛṣṇa\index{Krsna@Kṛṣṇa},\footnote{\citeauthor[1993: 41]{endo1993}.} whereas Sant Sundardās was regarded as one of the most learned Dādūpanthīs\index{Dadupanth@Dādūpanth} and, in his writings, propagated the Sant belief system as a representative Vaiṣṇava\index{Vaisnava@Vaiṣṇava} \textit{bhakta}\index{bhakta@\textit{bhakta}}.\footnote{\citeauthor[2023: 84–87]{horstmann2023shrine}.}

It is noteworthy that two of the authors, namely Sundardās\index{Sundardas@Sundardās} and Nārāyaṇatīrtha\index{Narayanatirtha@Nārāyaṇatīrtha}, spent a significant part of their lives in Benares\index{Benares}. Sundardās lived in Benares between the first decade of the seventeenth century and around 1625 CE, where he was trained in the dominant knowledge systems of the time, including aesthetics and the tradition of artistic poetry (\textit{kāvya}\index{kavya@\textit{kāvya}}).\footnote{Ibid. p. 86.} \citeauthor{endo1993} (1993: 56) convincingly narrows the flourishing period of Nārāyaṇatīrtha to 1600–1690 CE. It is generally agreed that Nārāyaṇatīrtha also spent much of his life in Benares, though the exact period remains to be determined.\footnote{\citeauthor[2004: 24]{penna2004}.} It is possible that both authors lived in Benares simultaneously.

The few clues that exist for locating Rāmacandra and the \textit{Tattvayogabindu} first of all narrow the place of its composition to northern India. One of the most compelling reasons for this conclusion is the list of nine rivers in section \uproman{40}. Compared to Rāmacandra's primary source text, the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} mentions the rivers Vipāśā\index{Vipasa@Vipāśā} (modern Beas\index{Beas}) and Śatarudrā\index{Satarudra@Śatarudrā} (modern Sutlej\index{Sutlej}) instead of the central-southern Indian rivers Godāvārī\index{Godavari@Godāvarī} and Kāverī\index{Kaveri@Kāverī}, indicating a northern context.\footnote{See n. \ref{riversrivers} on p. \pageref{riversrivers}.} Although the \emph{Yogasvarodaya} was likely composed much further south, the currently identified reception evidence points particularly to eastern India. This observation is based on the fact that it is currently known only through specific citations, namely in the \textit{Prāṇatoṣiṇī}\index{Pranatosini@\textit{Prāṇatoṣiṇī}}, written near Calcutta,\footnote{\citeauthor{shastri1905} 1905.} the \emph{Yogakarṇikā}\index{Yogakarnika@\textit{Yogakarṇikā}}, whose origin is unknown,\footnote{The only available printed edition of the \emph{Yogakarṇikā} by \citeauthor{yogakarnika} (2004) is based on a manuscript presumably from Benares, see \citeauthor[2004: \lowroman{6}]{yogakarnika}.} and the \citetitle{shabdakalpadruma}, which Radhakanta Deva\index{Radhakanta Deva} (1784–1867) also compiled in Calcutta. Thus, eastern India appears to have been a significant area for the circulation of the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}.
Given that Rāmacandra extensively drew from the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} for the compilation of the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, and since his fifteen yogas were part of a literary discourse centred in Benares, perhaps also circulating orally, it is plausible that Rāmacandra also resided in Benares for some time. Finally, this hypothesis is supported by the fact that Sundaradeva\index{Sundaradeva} lived in Benares\footnote{\citeauthor[2018: 123]{birch2018proliferation}.} and his citations of the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} in the \emph{Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā}\index{Hathasanketacandrika@\textit{Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā}} constitute the second-oldest piece of transmitted textual evidence for the \emph{Tattvayogabindu}.\footnote{See p. \pageref{dating}.} Based on the cumulative evidence, the hypothesis that Rāmacandra compiled the \emph{Tattvayogabindu} in northern India is well supported. It seems possible that Rāmacandra may have spent some time of his life in the Benares area. 

What is certain is that both the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} and the \textit{Tattvayogabindu}, as has been shown,\footnote{For the dating of the \textit{Tattvayogabindu} and the \textit{Yogasvarodaya}, see p.~\pageref{dating}.} must have been written before 1591~CE. Thus, the complex taxonomies examined here emerged within a remarkably narrow timeframe---most likely from the middle of the sixteenth century to around 1690~CE---with a significant proportion of these texts produced in the discursive milieu of Benares.
Each author and text has individual reasons for addressing or codifying the complex yoga taxonomies. Rāmacandra's text, for instance, appears to have been intended for the education of young princes or potential kings. The taxonomy serves both as an encyclopaedia and a way to convey the superiority of Rājayoga, in the sense of a ``Yoga for Kings,'' and its methods through a hierarchical taxonomy. Integrating the fifteenfold yoga taxonomy from the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} can be understood as continuing an older Śaiva strategy described by \citeauthor[2019]{birch2019}. In this strategy, yogas from various traditions are integrated into a hierarchical schema that emphasises a Śaiva interpretation of \textit{samādhi} and liberation in life (\textit{jīvanmukti}\index{jivanmukti@\textit{jīvanmukti}}).\footnote{The Śaiva subordination of yogas into a Rājayoga model began after the tenth century CE and was first described by \citeauthor[2019]{birch2019}.} Fundamentally, this is an extension of the older fourfold taxonomy that subordinated Mantra, Laya, and Haṭhayoga in hierarchical order to Rājayoga.

In the case of the \textit{Yogasiddhāntacandrikā}, \citeauthor{endo1998} (1998: 34–35), drawing on Paul\index{Hacker, Paul} \citeauthor{hacker1979}'s concept of ``inclusivism'' (1979), interprets Nārāyaṇatīrtha's treatment as an attempt to incorporate the increasingly popular medieval yogas into a Pātañjala framework. In Endo's reading, forms of yoga that he characterises as inferior, foreign, and popular are not only equated with Pātañjalayoga but also subordinated to it.

Sundardās's \textit{Sarvāṅgayogapradīpikā} has a less instructional and more informal or encyclopaedic character, placing great emphasis on both systematics and aesthetics. However, it also follows a clear agenda much less hierarchical than those taxonomies that place Rājayoga at the top. Instead, Sundardās's taxonomy establishes a common thread and an overarching context among the yoga categories he discusses. He creates a harmonious order through a successive and logical sequence, beginning with Bhaktiyoga, with an evident Vaiṣṇava influence, moving through Haṭhayoga, encompassing various body-oriented yogas. His system culminates in philosophically oriented yogas, beginning with Sāṃkhyayoga, which creates awareness of duality through the philosophical contemplation of the world's components. This differentiation is developed further in Jñānayoga, in which the yogin recognises unity with the world, and then in Brahmayoga, which advances the experience of unity with the universe, ultimately leading to the state he considers the \textit{summum bonum}, Advaitayoga.\footnote{As \citeauthor{burger2014sarvangayogapradipika} (2014: 706) observed, Sundardās's three yoga tetrads strongly recall the tripartite division of yogas in the \emph{Bhagavadgītā} with Bhakti, Karma, and Jñāna.}

This categorical and interpretative diversity reflected in the complex taxonomies suggests a veritable cross-traditional yoga boom in the milieu of the authors considered, marking an unprecedented wave of popularity, particularly for medieval and body-oriented yoga forms such as Haṭhayoga. The materials examined here indicate that these forms attracted increasing interest among a range of traditions and members of the educated elite. At the same time, the comparison of the texts of the yoga taxonomies revealed a genuine struggle to assert interpretive authority, a contest over the interpretation and classification of yoga categories, and an attempt to highlight one's conception of the best yoga.
In this case study, within its clearly defined discursive framework, the previously mentioned popularity appears to have reached several social strata. Yoga and its efficacy were endowed with high soteriological significance, and the materials examined here suggest that by the sixteenth century yoga had become an established part of the educational repertoire in at least some ruling circles. Furthermore, it becomes evident that the meanings of the various yoga categories were very fluid and were discursively negotiated in lively and dynamic exchange processes. It is therefore not surprising that the phenomenon of early modern complex yoga taxonomies developed in a specific fertile ground. One such fertile ground was sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Benares, in eastern Uttar Pradesh in northern India.

Benares had long been an important centre of learning, attracting scholars from near and far for centuries. However, with the onset of Muslim rule in India, many prominent scholars teaching in Benares left the city for fear of religious persecution. Benares experienced a cultural revival during the sixteenth century, to which the relatively accommodating religious policies of the Mughal Emperor\index{Mughal Empire} Akbar\index{Akbar} and his immediate successors, often associated with the ``Pax Mughalia''.\footnote{In 1556, at the age of 13, Akbar ascended the throne of the Mughal Empire, which had been partially reconquered by his father but collapsed immediately after his death under almost hopeless circumstances; see \citeauthor[1989: 53]{stietencron1989}. Following a period of military consolidation, Akbar presided over a diverse empire in which social and religious tensions persisted. Some Hindu communities experienced subordination and exploitation, although the extent and character of these experiences varied across regions and periods. The degree of Mughal control over regions such as Rajasthan, Gujarat, and central India also changed over the course of his reign. Beginning with his marriage to the daughter of the Rajput prince Rājā Bihārī Mal of Amber in 1562, Akbar initiated a policy of pacification. More wives from the princely houses of Rajasthan followed, all of whom retained their religious customs. He issued decrees allowing Hindus to rebuild temples and perform religious practices in public. Akbar's reign is often characterised by a relatively accommodating religious policy, although its implementation varied across regions and contexts. For instance, he promoted Hindus like Todar Mal and Mān Singh to high political positions for the first time; see ibid., p. 70. Akbar encouraged interfaith dialogue and also sponsored the Dīn-i-Ilāhī, often translated as the ``Divine Faith''. This small, court-centred religious initiative has been interpreted as an attempt to draw on elements of different traditions while setting aside those regarded as unconvincing, in pursuit of a broader truth; its precise character and scope, however, remain debated; see ibid. p. 62. During this time, numerous previously destroyed Hindu temples were rebuilt in Benares; see ibid., pp. 58–59. Later vassals of the Mughal emperors, especially the later Kachwaha Rajput rulers of Amber, notably under Rāja Man Singh I, constructed numerous temples and \textit{ghāṭ}s in the city; see \citeauthor[2006: 493–495]{hooja2006}. Akbar's immediate successor, Jahāngīr\index{Jahangir@Jahāngīr} (1605–1627), continued many aspects of this relatively accommodating religious policy; see \citeauthor{jahangir1999} (1999). A shift towards a less accommodating imperial stance is often associated with the reign of Shāh Jahān\index{Shah Jahan@Shāh Jahān}, who ruled the Mughal Empire from 1628 to 1658, particularly from 1632 onwards. According to the cited scholarship, Shāh Jahān issued orders for the destruction of some Hindu temples under construction and for restrictions on the construction and renovation of temples of other religions, following advice from Islamic theologians. The extent to which these measures were enforced, and how far this shift affected the population after several decades of relatively accommodating policies and interreligious dialogue, remains unclear. The broader impact of these measures appears to have been limited, although the available evidence does not permit a precise assessment. During Shāh Jahān's reign, seven instances of temple destruction are documented; see \citeauthor{eaton2001}. The extent to which these measures affected Benares remains unclear; the available evidence does not suggest a major disruption there. For example, the then Rāja of Amber, Jai Singh I, who ruled from 1627 to 1667, was an important military ally, and his father, Man Singh I, had invested large sums in the construction of Hindu temples in Benares. Under Aurangzeb\index{Aurangzeb} (1658–1707), more explicitly anti-Hindu measures were reintroduced.} This was a significant catalyst not only for the consolidation of religious identities in northern India,\footnote{\citeauthor[2011]{ohanlon2011religious}.} but also for the emergence of an increasingly pronounced ``Hindu'' identity, which took shape during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.\footnote{\citeauthor[2006: 188]{clark2006}.} Yoga played a crucial role in the development of this new identity. The patronage and policies of the Mughal emperors, together with the activities of regional Rajput elites and scholars, appear to have helped Benares develop once again into a major centre of intellectual and religious exchange, attracting scholars from different regions, as illustrated by Sundardās and Nārāyaṇatīrtha. Concurrently, as \citeauthor{birch2020} (2020: 471–472) demonstrated in his article \citetitle{birch2020}, there was a general flourishing of Haṭhayoga literature in both North and South India. From the sixteenth century onwards, Haṭhayoga literature became increasingly diverse. Authors from various traditions, mainly learned Brahmins, sought to expand Haṭhayoga and integrate other yogas and religions. The beginnings of this development, highlighted by \citeauthor{birch2020}, are discernible here.

Another factor that may have contributed to the flourishing of Haṭhayoga literature was the consolidation of corporate identities among the four main ascetic orders of North India (Nāths\index{Nath order@Nātha order}, Daśanāmī saṃnyāsīs\index{Dasanami samnyasi@Daśanāmī saṃnyāsī}, Rāmānandīs\index{Ramanandi@Rāmānandī}, and Udāsīs\index{Udasi@Udāsī}). By 1600, the Nāth yogīs were reportedly organised into 12 panths, with all their headquarters located in North India.\footnote{\citeauthor[2011: 17]{mallinsonnath}.} This institutional consolidation may have increased the visibility and influence of their views, teachings, and texts within the religious landscape of northern India. This, in turn, may have encouraged other religious traditions to engage with their teachings and respond to their growing presence.    
 
Thus, the complex early modern yoga taxonomies examined here can be understood as emerging against the background of the convergence of various yogic traditions and associated religions within centres of intellectual and religious exchange during the early flourishing of a new, more diversified wave of yoga literature. Benares functioned as one important hub in the materials examined, while the likely southern provenance of the \textit{Yogasvarodaya} points to an additional geographical context for the circulation of these taxonomies.\footnote{Given the strong likelihood that the \emph{Yogasvarodaya} was composed in southern regions, this suggests that the discourse surrounding complex yoga taxonomies also had another area of dissemination. South Indian scholars were among those drawn to Benares. This is evidenced by the authors discussed here: on one hand, Nārāyaṇatīrtha, who was originally from Kaza in the Guntur District of present-day Andhra Pradesh and then spent a significant part of his life in Benares, see \citeauthor[1993: 43]{endo1993}. On the other hand, Sundaradeva, the author of the \emph{Haṭhasaṅketacandrikā}, also originated from more southern regions before moving to Benares, see \citeauthor[2018: 123 n. 61]{birch2018proliferation}.} Moreover, these texts constitute a literary testament to these processes, reflecting the discursive negotiations through which authors from different traditions competed for taxonomic and interpretive authority, adapted inherited materials, and repositioned yogic practices in response to new impulses.
